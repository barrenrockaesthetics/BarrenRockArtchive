# Salmon, サーモン, 鮭 (さけ)

```
Artists:
- "Zejing Rie Ye"
```

---

According to research, said Rie, human cells renew themselves every seven years. Having studied art and indulged in the local cuisine of Japan for a decade, Rie seemed to have acquired a "Japanese" body. Despite this cellular change, her legal identity remains unchanged. Later, she moved to the UK for further study, and started developing her ongoing series “breeding migration”, that explores themes of borders, identity, and mobility. In her work “breeding migration – made in…”, she transfers her UK visa onto salmon skin sourced from local fisher, and presents it on the tray table of a discarded airplane seat.

When I was small, I used to think salmon sashimi came from Japan, and the smoked ones came from Norway. But then, I heard that there was no salmon sushi in traditional Japanese restaurants because they were unable to manage parasites found in raw salmon. They started eating raw salmon only after farmed salmon was introduced and imported from Norway. Originally, I thought they never consumed salmon before the import. However, it turns out they had been eating cooked trout (鮭, さけ) for a considerable time. They even had names for different trouts. What a huge mistake. Being tossed to the other end of the globe, Norwegian salmon ironically became a cliché of Japanese cuisine.

Rie changed her skin in Japan, yet eventually drifted to the UK. Despite being an island nation akin to Japan, the UK is a net importer of fish. Could consuming these scales, both familiar and foreign, with her visa embedded, shed new light to her identity? “breeding migration – made in…” began as Zijing’s reflection on identity and freedom. Similar to the salmon's unpredictable migratory patterns and origins, her history exhibits a comparable degree of instability and disorder, yet in a different direction. I see her act of transferring her visa an attempt to recur salmon’s “change in nationality.” It sarcastically magnifies physical, psychological, and institutional differences in identity agency between the artist and salmon.

### Blanket, Skin, Home

Zijing also replicated her UK visa into a woven rug. It might be welcoming at home, but it becomes her skin when there is not a “proper” one— Slade School of Fine Art required Rie to clear their studios for exhibition spaces. Frustrated that the school gave only three weeks’ notice, Zijing responded with a work, “Holdout” (“Nail Household” [1]). Constructed from her studio materials, the piece forms a square “household” that is determined to occupy and keep her space. Her visa-blanket sits draped on a chair inside, keeping her place accompanied.

In “breeding migration exp. 2” at The Bomb Factory, Rie forcefully bends a Western-style balcony railing into a right angle— a desperate effort to hold its spatial form by using a singular piece of domestic architectural material. A vintage postcard depicting Chinese porcelain curls between the bars. A pair of pigeon-shaped glass bottles rest quietly within the iron railing. Two pairs of squid sake bottles (イカ德利 [2]) echoed. These sake bottles could also be grilled and serve as snacks, not leaving any trace. From afar, they resemble human figures, as if two quiet silhouettes standing before a window. This time, salmon skins are carefully stored in a half-open wooden cargo crate. The visa-blanket rests close by on a trolley, which had been used during the exhibition's setup. Ceramic-cast wheels scatter themselves throughout the space. Their new “skin” has taken up their voids within their bodies, dysfunctioning their mobility. Subtle tensions arise in the sculptural installation through minimal arrangements and combinations of elements. These tensions weave through past and present bodies, tendencies and stability, and texturized a space of empty, floating void.

### Flight

Contemporary air travel are predetermined courses— Luggages are sent to the conveyor belt and then received at the destination. After departing immigration, one enters a non-place where no residents are permitted, a space that feels nonexistent. Windows at the gate offer the sole glimpse beyond the capsulated passage. “No Trespassing” (exp. 3 of breeding migration) reassembles various decommissioned airplane parts into an installation. It is however enclosed with iron fencing, viewable only from the outside. This is a rare chance to meet all these aircraft components, but I could barely imagine what they were for. Rie describes them as having once carried the “promise of travel and freedom.” However, it's now obvious that they are no longer positioned for flight. How are they feeling to be stripped of their function and forced to reside within an abandoned industrial building? Why does the arched part, shining in this vacant dark, look like an ambient lamp to me? Where is the white-tiled box heading with its pallet trolley? The wooden crate— once used for storing fish skin— is positioned at the farest end. Observing through the fence, I could not confirm if the salmon skins were still there. Ocean has no borders. However, by packing fish into crates, humans have made them transcend national borders in the sky.

Rie’s choice of materials and arrangement are superb. She can always reassemble objects into relational joints, effortlessly sculpting the presence of space. Onishi Yoshinori once described his experience of reading a good Haiku, “I can see ‘姿’ (sugata, meaning form or posture) in a good haiku [3], like a shadow that moves with the shape, inseparable from it” (2019, p.17) [4].
